A Field Report From VoltLab

What a physics lab taught an IT club about measurement, noise, and knowing when the hardware is lying.

BY TASNIM ANJUM · VoltLab lead

VoltLab was supposed to be the easy project. Read an ADC, plot a curve, fit an exponential. The hardware worked on the bench for a week straight. Then we put it in a real physics lab, and the 'easy' project spent a month teaching us about noise.

The first lie: the ADC

Our first builds showed a beautiful exponential decay — until we checked it against a multimeter and found the curve was shifted by a constant. The ADC's reference voltage was not what the datasheet implied, and every sample carried that error. The lesson: measure your instrument before you trust it.

raw = adc.read()
real = raw * scale + offset   # offset measured, not assumed
smooth = moving_average(real, window=16)

The second lie: the room

On the bench, mains hum was invisible. In the lab, fluorescent lights and a dozen phone chargers added noise at 50 Hz and 100 Hz. The fix was not more filters — it was a moving average over one full mains cycle, and a note in the manual telling students not to charge phones next to the board.

The real lesson is that engineering is the discipline of asking 'is this number real?' A measurement pipeline is only as honest as its least-trusted stage, and the cheapest way to find a lying stage is to check it against a second instrument.

A measurement pipeline is only as honest as its least-trusted stage.

The physics department has now run three batches of first-year labs on VoltLab. The constant-offset story is in the lab manual, and one student found a genuinely new way to fool it last semester — which is, honestly, the best outcome we could ask for.
